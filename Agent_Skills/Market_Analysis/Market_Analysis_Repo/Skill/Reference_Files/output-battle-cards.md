# Output template: Battle cards

Use this structure to produce one battle card per competitor. Battle cards are the most actionable deliverable — they're what the user (or their sales team) reaches for when they're in a conversation with a prospect who's also considering a competitor.

---

## Philosophy

A battle card is honest or it's useless. If a competitor is genuinely better at something, the card says so — because the prospect already knows, and pretending otherwise destroys credibility.

A good battle card:
- Tells you exactly what to say when a competitor's name comes up
- Warns you about situations where you're likely to lose
- Gives you the ammunition to win where you can
- Helps you handle objections with substance, not spin

A bad battle card:
- Pretends the competitor has no strengths
- Lists weaknesses without evidence
- Provides generic "our product is better" talking points
- Ignores the scenarios where the competitor actually wins

## Structure (one card per competitor)

```
# Battle card: [Competitor Name]

**Last updated**: [Date]
**Threat level**: [High / Medium / Low] — based on market position, trajectory, and overlap with your target customer

## Quick profile

[2-3 sentences: what they are, who they serve, what they're known for, and their trajectory. This is what you say when someone asks "who is [Competitor]?"]

## Pricing summary

| Their plan | Price | Best comparison to your offering |
|-----------|-------|--------------------------------|
| [Tier 1] | [Price] | [How it compares to your equivalent] |
| [Tier 2] | [Price] | [same] |
| [Tier 3] | [Price] | [same] |

**Pricing model**: [Per seat / usage / flat — and what it means in practice]
**Pricing angle**: [Are they cheaper? More expensive? Different value metric?]

## Their strengths (be honest)

These are the things they're genuinely good at. Your prospect may bring these up — don't dismiss them.

1. **[Strength]**
   - What it is: [One sentence]
   - Why it matters to the prospect: [Why they'd care]
   - Your response: [How you handle this — NOT "we're better," but what you actually offer instead, or how you frame the tradeoff]

2. **[Strength]**
   - Same structure

3. **[Strength]**
   - Same structure

## Their weaknesses (backed by evidence)

These are not guesses — they're patterns from customer reviews, community discussions, and product analysis.

1. **[Weakness]**
   - What it is: [One sentence]
   - Evidence: [Where this came from — "consistent theme in G2 reviews" / "multiple Reddit threads mention this" / "visible gap on product page"]
   - How to surface it: [What question to ask the prospect to make them discover this themselves, rather than you just claiming it]
   - Impact on the customer: [Why this weakness actually matters in daily use]

2. **[Weakness]**
   - Same structure

3. **[Weakness]**
   - Same structure

## How to win against them

Specific scenarios and arguments:

**When the prospect values [thing your product does well]:**
[Talking points — be specific. "Our automation engine runs X% faster" is better than "we have better automation."]

**When the prospect is comparing on price:**
[If you're cheaper: frame the value clearly. If you're more expensive: here's how to justify the premium. If pricing models differ: here's how to reframe the comparison.]

**When the prospect is already using them:**
[Migration pitch — acknowledge switching costs, offer migration support, highlight what they're missing. Reference specific complaints from Wave 2 data.]

**Best discovery questions to ask:**
These questions help the prospect identify needs that you serve better:
- "[Question that reveals a pain point the competitor causes]"
- "[Question that highlights a workflow your product handles better]"
- "[Question about scale, integration, or support that exposes a gap]"

## When they win (acknowledge it)

Be explicit about the scenarios where this competitor is the better choice. This builds credibility and helps your team qualify prospects better — there's no point chasing deals you'll lose.

**They win when:**
- [Scenario 1 — e.g., "The prospect needs deep [integration X] and our integration is basic"]
- [Scenario 2 — e.g., "The prospect is a large enterprise needing SOC2 compliance, which we don't have yet"]
- [Scenario 3 — e.g., "The prospect has a small budget and their free tier is genuinely good enough"]

**Qualifying question:** [What question should you ask early to determine if this is a deal you're likely to lose?]

## Common objections and responses

When a prospect says one of these things, here's how to respond:

**"[Competitor] is the market leader / everyone uses [Competitor]"**
Response: [Acknowledge the position, then pivot — what problems has market leadership created? Are they resting on their laurels? What are newer entrants doing better?]

**"[Competitor] has more features"**
Response: [Feature count ≠ quality. Which features matter for their specific use case? Are those "features" actually well-built?]

**"[Competitor] is cheaper"**
Response: [If true: what's the total cost of ownership including time, integrations, and support? If not true: show the actual math.]

**"We're already using [Competitor] and switching is a pain"**
Response: [Acknowledge the switching cost. What's the cost of staying — growing frustration, pricing increases, missing capabilities? Offer migration support specifics.]

**"[Specific feature] that [Competitor] has and you don't"**
Response: [If you plan to build it: timeline and why. If you don't: why your approach is different and what it enables instead. If it's a real gap: be honest — "you're right, we don't have that yet."]

## Key vulnerability

The single biggest opening against this competitor. This is the one thing that, if you can make the prospect feel it, changes the conversation.

**Vulnerability**: [One sentence — clear and specific]
**Evidence**: [What data supports this]
**How to exploit**: [What to do with this information — a specific question, demo flow, or talking point]

## Strategic trajectory

Where this competitor is heading in the next 6-12 months based on Wave 3 signals:
- [What their hiring suggests they're building]
- [What their funding suggests about their next move]
- [How this changes the competitive dynamic]

This helps your team anticipate — not just react to — competitive moves.

---
*Confidence: [Overall confidence in this card — High if based on strong data across all waves, Medium if some areas are thin, Low if mostly estimates]*
```

## Important notes

- Update battle cards when anything material changes — a competitor raises funding, ships a major feature, changes pricing, or gets acquired.
- The "how to surface it" instructions for weaknesses are critical. Telling a prospect "their onboarding sucks" is less effective than asking "how long did it take your team to get up and running?" and letting them discover the pain themselves.
- Battle cards are internal documents. They should be candid in a way that external marketing can't be. That's their value.
- If the user's product doesn't exist yet, frame the "how to win" sections as product requirements — what would the product need to beat this competitor?
